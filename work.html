Standing up an AI enablement business unit

Context

In 2026, the organization consolidated documentation, training, and creative production into a new CXO-sponsored unit, the Applied Intelligence Academy, backed by the CEO and COO. I moved in to lead Documentation and Training, reporting to the CXO. The unit inherited signed client training revenue and a mandate to grow it.

The problem

Three capabilities that had operated as internal cost centers now had to sell, to delivery teams and to external government and enterprise clients, with no defined offering, no engagement models, and no clear boundary against adjacent teams.

What I did

30+

services across documentation, learning, and creative production

7

5

  • Designed the unit’s service catalog: 30+ services across documentation, learning, and creative production, each written from the buyer’s problem outward rather than from our capabilities inward.
  • Packaged them into seven bundles that solve a whole problem, so clients buy outcomes instead of hours.
  • Defined five engagement models, from fixed-scope to subscription to multi-phase programs, and a single-catalog rule: internal work builds the case studies external clients ask for.
  • Positioned the offering for where the market is heading: AI-ready knowledge, agent-facing documentation, QA standards for AI-generated content.

02

Building the knowledge infrastructure for a scaling AI company

The problem

Knowledge lived on individual laptops and in chat threads. When someone left, what they knew left with them. Documentation was thin and mostly outdated; there were no templates, no naming conventions, no version control, no archival. An audit failed on exactly those points: documents that couldn’t be shown as authored, maintained, versioned, or retired.

What I did

4

layers, one source of truth

  • Built a SharePoint-based documentation ecosystem as the single source of truth across projects and teams: structured libraries, metadata, version history, review workflows, and an archival process so nothing simply disappeared.
  • Wrote the standards that made it stick: templates, file naming conventions, a style guide, and a document control process. Then ran the adoption, training teams on the system rather than publishing rules and hoping.
  • Added a reporting layer in Power BI so leadership could see team status, resource allocation, and project progress from live data instead of monthly slide decks.
  • Created a training tracking system where none existed, capturing cohorts, attendance, assessments, and client feedback, turning training from an activity into a measured service.
  • Piloted an AI layer on top: Copilot Studio agents over the SharePoint knowledge base, so project status questions can be asked in plain language and answered from the documents themselves.

“What started as a fix for a failed audit became the operating standard.”

Result

200+

team members adopted the system

200+ team members adopted the system. The next audit found everything in place: authored, versioned, maintained, archived. What started as a fix for a failed audit became the operating standard for how the company’s delivery teams document, track, and report their work.

03

Human-authored, AI-checked: a content operations model for an AI company

The problem

The pressure in 2024–25 was to let AI write everything. For documentation of proprietary AI and data platforms, that’s the wrong trade: fast drafts full of plausible errors, reviewed by people who didn’t write them. Meanwhile, the real bottleneck was never the first draft. It was everything after it: proofreading, accuracy checks, style consistency across writers.

“The real bottleneck was never the first draft.”

What I did

  • Inverted the default. First drafts of product documentation stay human; the writer who understands the product owns the words. AI enters as a peer reviewer: language, accuracy against source, adherence to the style guide.
  • Put AI where the time actually went. Proofreading, terminology consistency, style enforcement, and quality checks became AI-assisted steps in the workflow, with the writer keeping final sign-off.
  • Worked inside the guardrails, not around them. Built the workflow on IT-approved tools only (Microsoft Copilot, Grammarly), and am drafting the team’s AI usage standard, covering approved tools, what content can and cannot go into AI, and review requirements. Currently in pilot.
  • Applied the same model to media. Explainer videos and microlearning run through an AI-assisted pipeline (Canva, Copilot, Camtasia) with the same rule: human narrative, AI-accelerated production.

Result

Proofreading and accuracy-check time dropped sharply. Documents across the team read like one company wrote them. Quality went up without a single AI-drafted claim shipping unreviewed.

04

Writing inside the rules: UX content for Wells Fargo’s digital banking redesign

2014–2016 · San Francisco · Wells Fargo

Context

Wells Fargo was redesigning its digital banking all at once: online banking on desktop, the tablet and smartphone apps, and a new app for Apple Watch. Same customers, same accounts, four very different screens. I was the content strategist on the customer experience team, onsite in San Francisco, responsible for the words on those screens.

The problem

A bank’s interface is regulated text. Every label, error message, and disclosure has to be accurate, compliant, accessible, and ready for translation, and it has to hold up across four screen sizes, the smallest of which shows a few words at a time. Design, product, business analysis, engineering, and legal each had a stake in the words, and each worked from its own documents. On a program that size, the risk is that copy gets decided last and reviewed in pieces.

What I did

4

platforms: desktop, tablet, smartphone, Apple Watch

5

partner functions in every copy review

2

years onsite, Nov 2014 to Dec 2016

  • Made the copy deck the single source of truth. I turned business and technical requirements into copy decks. Every screen was broken into components (masthead, header, body, disclosures, calls to action), with desktop, tablet, and smartphone copy side by side, conditional messages marked, hidden accessibility text written out, and every error, warning, and success message coded and listed. Design, engineering, and Legal reviewed the same document instead of three versions of it.
  • Wrote for four screen sizes, down to the wrist. A sentence that reads well on desktop has to shrink to a phrase on a phone. On Apple Watch, the job was an at-a-glance view of a customer’s finances: a few words per screen, read in a second, with no room for ambiguity and no slack on legal accuracy.
  • Worked the review loop with Legal and Compliance. I partnered with UI/UX designers, business analysts, product and project managers, developers, and Legal/Compliance, so each screen’s copy matched the product, met the requirement, and could be approved.
  • Wrote accessibility and translation into the first draft. I drafted with cross-functional global teams so the copy met accessibility requirements and worked for in-language translation from the start.
  • Put the words in front of real customers. The bank brought customers in to use the redesigned apps and react live. Those sessions showed where a label, instruction, or message made people hesitate, and the fixes went back into the copy deck before launch.
  • Planned the content, then led the people writing it. I ran a current-state content analysis to set the content roadmap and led a UX content team working across the bank’s lines of business.
“Catching all the little items in a copy deck.”
Brigid M, Communications Pod Manager, Wells Fargo · Endorsements

Figure 04

Result

The redesigned apps shipped on desktop, tablet, smartphone, and Apple Watch. Late in the program, with other leads out on planned leave, I was asked to step in with almost no background, take over copy deck finalization, and baseline the final deliverable for the customer experience team. The senior producer’s verdict: without that, the program would not have stayed on schedule.

What it became

In 2019 I brought the same model to Presight: a content style guide, a copy document as the place where on-screen words get reviewed and approved, localization, and content testing, pitched to product and engineering as one process. In 2021 I formalized it with a professional development certification in UX Writing for Technical Writers (UX Writers Collective).

Case studies 1 to 3 are about building systems. This is where I learned why they matter. When every word has an owner, a reviewer, and a rule, the only way to ship on time is to have one place where the words live.

One standard, every market: rewriting Pfizer’s SOPs for a global Oracle migration

2006–2008 · Chennai and Sydney · Pfizer

Context

Pfizer was moving its global financial shared services off a patchwork of legacy systems and onto one Oracle platform, market by market. Every process that changed needed its procedures rewritten to a single Pfizer standard, used the same way in every business unit worldwide. I led the technical writers in the documentation team at Pfizer’s solutions center in Chennai. Our work was primarily for the Australia and New Zealand markets, with client teams in New York and Dublin.

The problem

Each legacy system came with its own documents, written its own way. As markets moved to Oracle, SOPs, work instructions, and forms described processes that no longer existed, or described the same process differently from one country to the next. In a regulated company, a controlled document that doesn’t match the process is an audit finding waiting to happen. Development and delivery teams on three continents were also following their own versions of the software development lifecycle.

What I did

120+

SOPs written

27

technical writers led

832

legacy documents in the migration

  • Rewrote the processes around Oracle, one SOP at a time. Worked with process owners and subject matter experts across business units to write 120+ SOPs, plus the work instructions, forms, and templates that go with them, each describing the new Oracle process to Pfizer’s global document standards.
  • Ran each document through control, not just through a writer. Designed the templates for functional and technical designs, checked every document for accuracy and completeness before it was routed, and moved it through SME, quality, and process-owner approval in Pfizer’s Documentum-based system until it became effective.
  • Led the legacy migration. On an 832-document migration, the team reclassified legacy processes, SOPs, work instructions, standards, guidelines, and templates under Pfizer’s new classification standard, restructured and rewrote them, retired what was obsolete, and kept a cross-reference index so every old document pointed to its replacement.
  • Gave every team one lifecycle. Launched an SDLC website in April 2008 so development and delivery teams in India, Ireland, and the US followed the same process, with the same SOPs and deliverables.
  • Set the quality bar for 27 writers. Review checklists for SOPs and design documents (purpose and scope stated clearly, active voice, numbered steps, process maps that match the text), a team lead handbook, and a weekly delivery report to Pfizer’s documentation manager.

Figure 05

Result

The new SOPs went live as controlled, effective documents, each signed off by the process owner and the quality team. The SDLC website and its SOPs launched in April 2008. By the end of the migration in December 2008, 350 documents had been converted to the new standard and were effective in the document system.

What it became

Standards, templates, document control, and one repository everyone uses: the same model I rebuilt at Presight ten years later (case study 2). This is where I learned that standards only work if someone owns their adoption.

“Building a seamless process for quick and quality delivery.”
Ganga R, Documentation Manager, Pfizer · Endorsements
